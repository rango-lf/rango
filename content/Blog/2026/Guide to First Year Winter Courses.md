> [!info] The following guide only covers the courses I took as an MIE student.

At some point in your first year, you will find at least one person who claims that winter semester will be easier than fall semester. This is true in the sense that after fall semester, you will likely be better equipped with the skills and discipline to tackle your academic demands, but I personally found winter semester much more difficult in content and marking.

As established in my previous blog post, I will start with an introduction to the material, how grades are assessed, a bit about my experience, and my tips on surviving it. Please keep in mind that my experience/opinions are not universal, and you need to consider what is best for you personally to succeed academically.

## APS106 — Fundamentals of Computer Programming
#### Overview

APS106 is an introductory course to the Python programming language. You'll cover expressions and operators, data types, functions, loops, object-oriented programming, and more. The course involves take-home labs, in-person labs, weekly Quercus reflection surveys, two midterms, and one final exam.
#### Thoughts
If you have prior experience with Python, this class is quite easy and arguably skippable. Even if you didn't have any exposure to Python or you're rusty (like I did), this course is usually regarded as the easiest in winter semester. The in-person labs involve going to your assigned practical and completing various problems within a time limit. These can be stress-inducing--I found that the anxiety prevented me from performing as well as I could, even when I was very familiar with the content.

That said, this course is really set up for you to succeed. Instructors are incredibly responsive, resources are abundant on Quercus, there are in-lecture review sections prior to tests, and the teaching team always posts past term tests and exams for your reference. Sebastian Goodfellow was the coordinator in my year, and I thought he was excellent. The weekly reflections are easy free marks and shouldn't be overlooked.
#### Tips
- Try to complete take-home labs on your own without the help of generative AI. It will better ingrain the skills you need for the tests.
- Stay on top of the weekly reflections--they take very little time and are easy marks.
- During in-person labs, save your progress and submit to Gradescope frequently. Don't leave it to the last minute.
- Don't be afraid to ask TAs for help during in-person labs; many of them will guide you toward a correct answer rather than just give it to you.
- Past midterms and finals from 2017 onwards are very helpful for studying.
- Attend tutorials regularly. I found these immensely helpful for preparing for exam-level coding problems.
## APS112 — Engineering Strategies & Practice II
#### Overview
APS112 is very similar to APS111, but this time your team will work with an individual client on a unique problem. Your tutorial will also involve an Engineering Manager--a professor, industry expert, or similar--who oversees the progress of your project and your team's relationship with the client. Unlike APS111, you will present your project in front of an EM and CI (and possibly your client) during the final exam period. There is also a midterm near the end of the semester. Teams are assigned dedicated roles, such as Project Manager and Communication Coordinator.
#### Thoughts
Most people find this a little easier than APS111, since the first half is essentially the same; Team Charter, Project Requirements, Conceptual Design Specifications, and so on. The latter half is often difficult and requires significant planning ahead to succeed, as it involves the Measure of Success, carrying out the Measure of Success (i.e. testing your solution), and Final Presentation. My team this semester was significantly better than my fall one. They took initiative and I didn't feel like I was dragging everyone along. Most people have learned from their shortcomings in the previous semester, and most people will get a better team than they had prior.

My client was an upper-year engineering student who was flexible and easy to work with. That said, he didn't provide particularly solid or well-justified objectives and constraints, which made designing around them a challenge. The final presentation wasn't terribly difficult for me, though I suspect some of that comes down to being comfortable with public speaking.
#### Tips
- Most of the tips from the APS111 section of my previous post apply here.
- Lectures are less critical than in APS111, but I'd still recommend attending--the content will likely show up on the final test.
- Collect the free PD points. There seemed to be more opportunities in the winter semester than the fall.
- Don't hesitate to contact your client, but run any communications through your Engineering Manager first.
- Give yourself enough time to rehearse your final presentation multiple times. Pay attention to the small details--hand-offs, body language, timing.
- Even if you're not the Project Manager, make sure you understand how to create and interpret Gantt Charts. This showed up on the final test.
## MIE100 — Dynamics
#### Overview
Mandatory for Mech, Indy, ECE, and TrackOne students, MIE100 is the sequel to CIV100--but focused on objects in motion rather than static ones. The course covers kinematics and kinetics of particles, kinematics and kinetics of rigid bodies, and vibrations. Grades are based on WileyPlus assignments (with unlimited attempts), a quiz early in the semester, one midterm, and a final exam. All assessments are [Type C](https://undergrad.engineering.utoronto.ca/exams/exam-types-permitted-calculators/): digital or handwritten aid sheets are permitted.
#### Thoughts
I generally found this easier than CIV100, though I fumbled the final exam after burning out from the APS112 presentation. This course can be hit-or-miss--I met plenty of people who found it easier than CIV100 and plenty who found it significantly harder. Prof. Jazinizadeh was the coordinator in my year and is an excellent teacher; she provides her own blank notes that you can follow along with during lectures. The teaching team also publishes a solid collection of past quizzes, midterms, and finals on Quercus. My TAs were unengaging and not particularly well-informed, so I stopped attending tutorials after a while.

The latter half of the course--kinetics of rigid bodies and vibrations--is where most people struggle. The content gets much more difficult and, in my experience, most people tend to be disengaged after the midterm. Unlike CIV100, questions on assessments are generally not recycled from past exams.
#### Tips
- Keep up with the WileyPlus assignment due dates.
- Don't cheat on the assignments. They're harder than the actual test problems, and if you can work through them yourself, the exams become much more manageable.
- Like CIV100, the best way to learn is through frequent practice.
- Do past midterms and finals in an exam-like environment--no distractions, no shortcuts.
- Since questions aren't recycled, putting past exam questions on your cheat sheet isn't particularly useful. Focus on equations, concepts, and common problem-solving approaches instead.
- Pay close attention to the second half of the course. Don't let your guard down after the midterm.
## MAT187 — Calculus II
#### Overview
MAT187 continues from MAT186/APS162, covering methods of integration, first and second order differential equations, complex numbers, sequences and series, and parametric equations and polar calculus. In my year, PCE (pre-class essentials) quizzes and assignments were removed entirely due to academic dishonesty in previous years. Due to this, grades were based almost entirely on two midterms, a universal make-up test, and the final exam.
#### Thoughts
This was the hardest course I've taken so far. I took APS162 over the summer, so it had been four months since I last worked through calculus by the time this course started. On top of that, I found the flipped classroom approach--where you're expected to learn material through pre-readings before attending lectures focused on practice problems--to not work for me at all. Without much direct explanation of the theory, I often felt underprepared going into class. I would look around the lecture hall and see many of my classmates sharing the same confused faces. 

The low averages weren't surprising given the assessment structure and marking standards. Tests required full written explanations of your reasoning, and the marking was strict in my opinion. Incomplete explanations could mean a zero for the entire question. I wasn't alone in struggling with this course, which was at least somewhat reassuring, but it was a genuinely difficult semester for a lot of people.
#### Tips
- If you're struggling, reach out to your professors early--office hours exist for a reason. Don't wait until things are already bad like I did.
- Attend tutorials and participate. The tutorial questions may closely resemble those on the term tests, and TAs are generally quite good.
- Keep up with the weekly practice problems. They're a reliable indicator of what to expect on assessments.
- Try to work through practice problems without help at first. If you get stuck, generative AI can be useful for getting unstuck--just prompt it to guide you rather than hand you the answer.
- When practicing, write out your explanations and solutions like you would in an assessment. 
- If you took APS162 over the summer, make sure you review the material (particularly integration) before the semester starts. 
## ECE110 — Electrical Fundamentals
#### Overview
The first half of ECE110 focuses on physics--electromagnetism, electrostatic force, and related topics--while the second half shifts to circuits, including nodal and loop analysis and DC/AC circuits. The course involves bi-weekly in-person labs, WileyPlus assignments, two midterms, and a final exam. All assessments are [Type C](https://undergrad.engineering.utoronto.ca/exams/exam-types-permitted-calculators/): digital or handwritten aid sheets are permitted.
#### Thoughts
I dropped ECE110 after the first midterm to focus on MAT187, so I can't speak to the full course experience. From what I do know: the labs were easy and practically a guaranteed 100%, though the TAs were hard to track down and the labs themselves were fairly busy. The WileyPlus assignments were on the longer side, typically 18 to 20 questions, though mostly straightforward. After student pushback, the attempt limit per question was raised from 3 to 5.

Based on what my friends who completed the course told me, the content and structure are somewhat similar to CIV100 in terms of how you should approach studying.
#### Tips
- Study practice problems and past midterms/finals--this course rewards that approach, similar to CIV100.
- Past exam questions are generally not recycled, unlike CIV100.
- Make sure you understand how to derive equations and the theory behind the concepts. This was tested in my year's term test.
- The textbook is a decent resource for explanations and practice, but you don't need to go too deep into it according to friends who completed the course.
## MIE191 — Introduction to Mechanical and Industrial Engineering
#### Overview
MIE191 is a weekly seminar course where professors and alumni from the MIE department come in to speak about their experiences and work in various fields. Lectures are optional, but two written Quercus reflections are mandatory to receive credit.
#### Thoughts
This course was a welcome break between the more demanding required courses, and genuinely gave me some motivation to keep going. The talks are interesting, the speakers provide useful perspective on where engineering can take you, and you get the opportunity to connect with alumni and professors--which may come in handy later for research positions or internship references.
#### Tips
- Attend when you can. The talks are worth your time, and the networking opportunities can lead to future research and work positions.
- Complete the written reflections on time. They're easy and shouldn't take more than an hour.
## Conclusion
At this point, you're almost at the finish line. Keep going--the end is just ahead.

There's a common saying among upper-year engineering students: if you can get through first year, you can get through anything. I'm not sure I fully believed that going in, but having come out the other side, I think there's something to it. The workload is tough, the marking is often unforgiving, and there will be weeks where everything seems to hit at once. But the skills you build in managing your time and taking responsibility of your learning will reward you in the future.

Good luck with the rest of your year.
