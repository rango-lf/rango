If you're on this page, I presume it is because you want to learn more about me as a person/idea/philosophy/enigma/social experiment/etc. aside from my academic and professional pursuits. **This page will more-or-less be a long, slightly self-obsessed ramble about the stories and views that have shaped who I am and my approach to engineering.**

I was raised and educated in Richmond, BC, a municipality within the Metro Vancouver area that is best known for fishing, agriculture, Chinese food, bad drivers, and a lot of old people. I did not have a particularly remarkable childhood. I grew up in a crappy old flat with my Cantonese maternal grandparents and mother, while my Malaysian biological father was out of the picture. From adolescence and onward, I have been adopted into my step-father's family, whom I love and treasure deeply.

Both my mother and step-father attended the University of British Columbia, where they studied biochemistry and civil engineering respectively. My older step-brother graduated from UBC recently with a degree in French and computer science. My older step-sister, arguably the smartest sibling, is currently studying engineering physics at the same institution. As a result, the clear path for me was to attend the University of Toronto out of spite of the family tradition (this is a joke, mostly).

I spent most of my life online, as is the case for the majority of young people in my generation. In the real world, I was quite lonely, isolated, and self-loathing due to bullying and ostracization, so staring at computers for hours at a time was my escape. Through the internet, I developed intense fixations on various topics, including the Chernobyl nuclear disaster. I watched (and rewatched) the HBO mini-series _Chernobyl_ (2019), I wrote, produced, and narrated a podcast about Chernobyl, I completed various school projects on nuclear energy, and I devoured any books about nuclear engineering and its history that I could get my hands on. This "autism-fueled obsession" (as my friends like to put it) ended up being the catalyst for me to seriously pursue engineering.

A frequently asked question is why I have (currently) chosen to pursue a career in academia rather than industry. I have always been cynical towards traditional, 9-to-5 office jobs. I have never strived towards the prospect of becoming another cog in a corporate machine; a "wageslave" as some may say. Academia has its more than fair share of problems, though I believe it will offer me greater opportunities to contribute to society--particularly through teaching younger generations--rather than filling the pockets of shareholders. So I guess this is the kind of job that won't make me go into an existential crisis, and they also say that being a teacher keeps you young and spirited. (We'll see if this statement still holds up in the next decade or so.)

Education and pedagogy has always been a passion, and grievance, of mine beginning from birth. Both my mother and grandparents were educators in the public education system. Ever since I could think for myself, I have been incredibly critical of the state of modern education: its lack of accessibility, exclusion of marginalised groups, detrimental effects on student mental well-being, and highly capitalistic, paternalistic nature. In school, I coasted through academics without much issue, but often felt confined by its demands of obedience, conformity, and "normal-ness" from students. I was a weird, neurodivergent kid who felt sandwiched between controlling, authoritarian teachers and mean, exclusionary peers. It did not seem like school was made for people like me.

I can vividly remember laying on my bedroom floor, furiously writing in my journal about how I would change the education system if I was the one in charge. In that world, my peers wouldn't tease me for my, or other students' disabilities, learning would be fun rather than tedious, the education system would challenge me to think for myself rather than mold me into the shape of a perfect employee. I hope that, in a position of authority within engineering post-secondary education, I can create the equitable, student-focused, and accessible vision that my younger self imagined. 

If it wasn't already obvious, I have been labelled as leftist and/or progressive. I suppose these are relatively accurate terms, though I don't really think they hold any weight without explanation. I generally see, interpret, and critique the world from a socialist, feminist, pacifist/anti-war, pro-science, and democratic perspective. What this means practically is:
1. I am broadly against reactionary, fear-based politics that are not based in evidence and/or reality--from both the right and left. I am against censorship (but not consequences), fascism, discrimination, and authoritarianism. 
2. I am generally for EDI policies in institutions such as academia, but I do not think they are a sufficient substitution for addressing material inequality, worker rights, and systemic oppression. Without taking a more radical and effective approach, these efforts can often come off as superficial and tokenising. 
3. I strongly believe that the engineering profession must become more radically pacifist, anti-war, and anti-imperialist if we are to truly uphold our commitment of serving humanity. Particularly, I condemn our contributions to weapon and aerospace systems with the goal of destruction, injury, and death.
4. Inevitably, almost all areas of engineering work involve ethical concerns. I challenge engineers to evaluate how we can better uphold said ethics and minimise threats to human welfare while also technologically advancing humanity. 
5. I am generally pro-AI, pro-automation, and anti-anti-AI from a democratic socialist point of view. I am optimistic and excited about its usages in advancing research, improving accessibility, creative work, and freeing people from inhumane work. That being said, I am staunchly against its misuse by bad actors (e.g. companies, techno-feudalists, warlords, Elon Musk-wannabes). 
6. Yes, I have read [*Das Kapital* by Karl Marx](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/download/pdf/Capital-Volume-I.pdf). No, I am not a Marxist.

I think I have advertised my political manifesto a bit excessively by now. So instead, I'll talk about the things that interest me personally. 

I enjoy almost all genres of music: R&B, indie, rock, folk, hip-hop, pop, alt, and many more. I am not a purist of any sort, and I am honestly just a fan of anything that sounds good. My favourite musicals include Hadestown, Hamilton, EPIC: The Musical, Mean Girls, Heathers, and Dear Evan Hansen. I love reading anything; I eat up encyclopedias, poetry, graphic novels, fiction, non-fiction, and fanfiction in equal measure.

When time allows, I draw digitally on my tablet. The subjects of my work are frequently original characters that I daydream about in my mind. Sometimes I get so fixated on these made-up, fictional characters that my mental wellness is called into question by friends and family. 

If it were a more economically feasible option, I would have loved to study in women and gender studies--particularly topics in manhood and masculinities. I spend a lot of my free time "investigating" how social media has influenced the image of contemporary masculinity and maleness, discrimination against female and queer masculinity, and gendered racism against Black and Brown men.
